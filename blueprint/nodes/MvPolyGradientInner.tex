\begin{lemma}[Pairing the gradient of a polynomial function against a vector]
Let $n\in\mathbb{N}$, let $P\in\mathbb{R}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and let
$y,z\in\mathbb{R}^n$, where $\mathbb{R}^n$ carries the Euclidean inner product.
Write $\widehat{P}(q)=P(q)$ for the polynomial function and $\pi_i\colon
\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, $\pi_i(w)=w_i$, for the $i$-th coordinate
projection. Then
\[
  \bigl\langle\nabla\widehat{P}(y),\,z\bigr\rangle
    =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,z_i ,
\]
and, with the same right-hand side read as the value at $z$ of the continuous
linear form $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,\pi_i$,
\[
  \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,\pi_i\Bigr)(z)
    =\bigl\langle\nabla\widehat{P}(y),\,z\bigr\rangle .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove the first identity and then deduce the second from it.

\emph{First identity.} By \noderef{MvPolyGradient}, the gradient of
$\widehat{P}$ at $y$ is the vector of partial derivatives,
\[
  \nabla\widehat{P}(y)=\bigl((\partial_1P)(y),\dots,(\partial_nP)(y)\bigr)
    \in\mathbb{R}^n .
\]
Substituting this into the left-hand side and expanding the Euclidean inner
product of two vectors coordinatewise --- for $a,b\in\mathbb{R}^n$ one has
$\langle a,b\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\,b_i$, the conjugation in the general
definition being trivial over $\mathbb{R}$ --- gives
\[
  \bigl\langle\nabla\widehat{P}(y),\,z\bigr\rangle
    =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,z_i ,
\]
which is the asserted identity; the two sums agree term by term, each pair of
terms differing at most by the commutativity of multiplication in $\mathbb{R}$.

\emph{Second identity.} The map $L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,\pi_i$ is a
finite linear combination of the coordinate projections, so it is a continuous
linear form on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and evaluating a linear combination of maps at a
point evaluates each summand there:
\[
  L(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,\pi_i(z)
    =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_iP)(y)\,z_i .
\]
By the first identity this last sum equals
$\langle\nabla\widehat{P}(y),z\rangle$, so
$L(z)=\langle\nabla\widehat{P}(y),z\rangle$, as claimed.
\end{proof}
